# 21. The Knowledge-Use Tensor

Combinatorial Maximalism asks how much lawful value can be obtained from admitted knowledge.

Let:

- $K$ be a knowledge element;
- $R$ a semantic role;
- $S$ an artifact or infrastructure surface;
- $L$ a lifecycle phase;
- $E$ an environment;
- $C$ a consumer;
- $V$ a version or compatibility target.

The projection space is:

$$
\mathcal{P} = K \times R \times S \times L \times E \times C \times V
$$

Roles include describe, classify, constrain, derive, generate, validate, plan, route, authorize, observe, diagnose, repair, simulate, explain, attest, replay, optimize, and evolve.

Surfaces include code, API, data, infrastructure, security, workflow, observability, documentation, evidence, and migration.

The goal is not to materialize every possible tuple. It is to identify projections that are:

- semantically entailed;
- operationally useful;
- bounded;
- validated;
- owned;
- receiptable.

A fact that a service processes restricted data may create many projections:

- encryption policy;
- IAM restrictions;
- logging redaction;
- region constraints;
- retention rules;
- deployment tests;
- incident severity;
- compliance evidence;
- consumer handling guidance.

The knowledge-use tensor exposes semantic waste. If the fact is manually restated in nine systems, the enterprise maintains nine chances for contradiction.

The ontology leverage ratio is:

$$
OLR =
\frac{validated\ consequences}
{independently\ maintained\ facts}
$$

A high ratio is valuable only when consequences remain correct and bounded. Unvalidated generation is not leverage; it is amplified error.

Combinatorial Maximalism therefore couples expansion with gates, receipts, and ownership. The enterprise seeks maximum lawful consequence, not maximum output volume.
